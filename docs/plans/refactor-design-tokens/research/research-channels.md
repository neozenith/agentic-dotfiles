# Colour as an Encoding Channel — theory, and how libraries operationalise it

Brief for a single hue-segmentation mechanism feeding both semantic node roles (Input/Process/Error) and categorical chart series. Every claim sourced; primary quotes where reachable. Fetched 2026-08-30.

## 1. Theory — the vocabulary

**1.1 Wilkinson — colour is not one channel.** The grammar's seven orthogonal classes are "Variables, Algebra, Geometry, Aesthetics, Statistics, Scales, Coordinates"; *Scales* "represent variables on measured dimensions", *Aesthetics* are "sensory attributes" ([Wikipedia on Wilkinson 2005](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilkinson%27s_Grammar_of_Graphics)). Table 10.1 decomposes colour into **hue, brightness, saturation** under *Surface* (alongside Form: position/size/shape/rotation/resolution; Motion; Sound; Text) — reproduced verbatim as "Table 1 … Adapted from Table 10.1 in [Wil05]", [arXiv:2510.06517](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.06517) p.3. ⚠️ **Primary text unreachable** (no open PDF; Google Books quota exhausted; archive.org search-inside dead). The three-way split is confirmed via that reproduction and via Wickham, who inherits it — Wilkinson's own prose is **unverified**.

**1.2 Wickham — the scale IS the mapping, and discrete colour defaults to evenly-spaced hue.**
> "A scale controls the mapping from data to aesthetic attributes … Scales are common across layers to ensure a consistent mapping from data to aesthetics." — [*A Layered Grammar of Graphics*](http://vita.had.co.nz/papers/layered-grammar.pdf) §3.2, p.12

> "we can map one variable to hue and another to saturation, to create a single aesthetic, color. We can also create **redundant mappings**, mapping the same variable to multiple aesthetics. This is particularly useful when producing a graphic that works in both color and black and white." — p.13

> "default scales will be added according to the aesthetic and **type of variable** … for color, continuous variables are mapped to a smooth path in the HCL color space, and **discrete variables to evenly spaced hues with equal luminance and chroma**. The choice of a good default scale is difficult." — p.15

`hue_pal()` is that mechanism, and its weaknesses are documented: "Maps each level to an evenly spaced hue on the colour wheel. **It does not generate colour-blind safe palettes**" (defaults `h=c(0,360)+15`, chroma `c=100`, luminance `l=65` — [scale_hue ref](https://ggplot2.tidyverse.org/reference/scale_hue.html)); it "works well for **up to about eight colours**, but after that it becomes hard to tell the different colours apart", and "because the colours all have the same luminance and chroma, they all appear as an identical shade of grey when printed in black and white" ([ggplot2 book 3e ch.11](https://ggplot2-book.org/scales-colour.html), which also re-exposes ColorBrewer's sequential/qualitative/diverging split via `scale_colour_brewer()`: "no palette is uniformly good for all purposes").

**1.3 Bertin — hue is selective + associative, but NOT ordered.** "Table I: Original Bertin" (Green, [*Toward a Perceptual Science of Multidimensional Data Visualization*](http://graphics.stanford.edu/courses/cs448b-06-winter/papers/Green_Towards.pdf) p.6) scores each retinal variable Associative / Selective / Ordered / Quantitative. Planar (x,y) = Yes/Yes/Yes/Yes; Size = –/Yes/Yes/Yes; Brightness = –/Yes/Yes/–; Texture = Yes/Yes/Yes/–; **Colour (hue) = Yes/Yes/–/–**; Orientation = Yes/Yes/–/–; Shape = Yes/–/–/–.

Both properties sit *inside* the nominal level: "Bertin subdivides the **nominal scale** level into two subcategories, associative and selective" (p.7); "Associate and selective perception provides only nominal scale classification … Bertin considers the planar variables, brightness, size and texture variables as ordered while **excluding shape, colour and orientation**" (pp.7–8). He also separates meaning from channel: reading starts with "**external identification**, where the viewer determines what components are being represented", then "**internal identification** … which components are mapped to which visual graphic variables" (p.5). Meaning lives *outside* the channel.

**1.4 Cleveland & McGill — hue is excluded from the accuracy ranking by construction.** The ordering is "1. Position along a common scale / 2. Positions along nonaligned scales / 3. Length, direction, angle / 4. Area / 5. Volume, curvature / **6. Shading, color saturation**" ([*JASA* 79(387), 1984](https://math.pku.edu.cn/teachers/xirb/Courses/biostatistics/Biostatistics2016/GraphicalPerception_Jasa1984.pdf) p.536). Hue is absent, and the stated reason is the whole thesis:

> "for example, **color hue** and texture (Bertin 1973) are two elementary tasks excluded from the list because **they do not have an unambiguous single method of ordering from small to large and thus might be regarded as better for encoding categories rather than real variables.**" — p.532

**1.5 Munzner — hue is an "identity channel".** Channels split two ways: "**Magnitude Channels: Ordered Attributes**" (position, length, tilt/angle, area, depth, colour luminance, colour saturation, curvature, volume) vs "**Identity Channels: Categorical Attributes**" (spatial region, **colour hue**, motion, shape) — [VAD Ch.5 slides, Fig 5.1](https://www.cs.ubc.ca/~tmm/courses/547-17/slides/marks-4x4.pdf). And: "first rule of color: do not talk about color! — color is confusing if treated as monolithic; decompose into three channels — ordered can show magnitude: luminance, saturation; **categorical can show identity: hue**" — [VAD Ch.10 slides](https://www.cs.ubc.ca/~tmm/courses/547-17/slides/color-4x4.pdf) sl.5.

**1.6 Tufte — colour's first job is to label.**
> "the fundamental uses of color in information design: **to label (color as noun)**, to measure (color as quantity), to represent or imitate reality (color as representation), and to enliven or decorate (color as beauty)." — *Envisioning Information* (1990) ch.5, p.81

> "For encoding abstract information, however, **more than 20 or 30 colors frequently produce not diminishing but negative returns.**" … "avoiding catastrophe becomes the first principle in bringing color to information: **Above all, do no harm.**" — *ibid.* p.81

The often-misattributed "smallest effective difference" — "Make all visual distinctions as subtle as possible, but still clear and effective", his "Occam's razor of information design" — is *Visual Explanations* (1997) p.73 ([trail](https://blas.com/visual-explanations/)), and it cuts *against* maximally saturated categorical palettes.

## 2. Practice — how shipped libraries encode the same rule

**2.1 ColorBrewer (Brewer; Harrower & Brewer 2003).** The typology is explicitly a measurement-level typology: "Colour can be used to imply categorical differences (e.g. forest, city, marsh) or to imply ordered differences (e.g. population density rates)" ([Harrower & Brewer, *Cartographic Journal* 40(1)](https://www.cs.rpi.edu/~cutler/classes/visualization/S18/papers/colorbrewer.pdf) p.29). The qualitative definition is the load-bearing one:

> "**Qualitative colour schemes rely primarily on differences in hue** to create a colour scheme that does not imply order, merely difference in kind. Since there is no conceptual ranking in nominal data it is inappropriate to imply order when depicting these data with colour … Qualitative schemes work best when **hue is varied and saturation and lightness are kept or nearly constant.** We do not recommend arbitrarily using strong 'neon' colours … and pastel colours … in the same qualitative colour scheme because these variations in saturation might imply order." — *ibid.* p.31

Sequential is the mirror: "**Lightness steps dominate** the look of these schemes" (p.29). Same rule, same words, nine years earlier: "Qualitative schemes use differences in hue to represent nominal differences … The lightness of the hues used for qualitative categories should be similar but not equal" (Brewer 1994, [*Color Use Guidelines*](https://web.natur.cuni.cz/~langhamr/lectures/vtfg1/mapinfo_2/barvy/colors.html)).

**Stated class ceiling:** "The maximum number of data classes ColorBrewer supports is **12**, although only a few of the colour schemes can be divided into this many steps and remain differentiable. As a general rule of thumb, cartographers **seldom use more than seven classes**" (p.32). Shipped data ([colorbrewer.json](https://colorbrewer2.org/export/colorbrewer.json)) confirms: only Paired and Set3 reach 12; Set1/Pastel1 = 9; Accent/Dark2/Pastel2/Set2 = 8.

**2.2 Vega-Lite — measurement level → scale type → palette, as executable config.** Types are defined by "[level of measurement]": `quantitative`, `temporal`, `ordinal`, `nominal`, `geojson` ([type docs](https://vega.github.io/vega-lite/docs/type.html)). For the `color` channel the default scale type is *Ordinal* for nominal/ordinal and *Linear* for quantitative ([scale docs](https://vega.github.io/vega-lite/docs/scale.html)). Then:

> "**Nominal fields use the `"categorical"` pre-defined named range (the `"tableau10"` scheme by default). Ordinal fields use the `"ordinal"` range (the `"blues"` scheme by default).** Quantitative and temporal fields use `"heatmap"` … for rect marks and `"ramp"` … for other marks." — *ibid.*

Vega's own taxonomy names the five families and their jobs: "**Categorical** color schemes can be used to encode discrete data values, each representing a distinct category"; **Sequential Single-Hue** and **Sequential Multi-Hue** "encode increasing numeric values"; **Diverging** for "a meaningful mid-point"; **Cyclical** for "periodic patterns" ([Vega schemes](https://vega.github.io/vega/docs/schemes/)).

**2.3 d3-scale-chromatic — the naming convention encodes the split.** "This module provides sequential, diverging and categorical color schemes … derived from Cynthia A. Brewer's ColorBrewer" ([README](https://github.com/d3/d3-scale-chromatic)). Sequential/diverging schemes are *arrays of arrays* parameterised by size *k* ("a size k ranging from 3 to 9" / "3 to 11") plus a continuous `interpolateX`; **categorical schemes are flat arrays with no k** — one fixed size ([docs](https://d3js.org/d3-scale-chromatic/categorical)). Category10/Tableau10/Observable10 = 10; Accent/Dark2/Pastel2/Set2 = 8; Set1/Pastel1 = 9; Paired/Set3 = 12.

**2.4 Observable Plot.** "Use the ***categorical*** type for categorical (nominal) unordered data, and the ***ordinal*** type for ordered data"; *categorical* "defaults to *observable10*"; type is inferred ("strings and booleans imply an *ordinal* scale; … anything else is *linear*"). Plot is candid that the distinction only bites on colour: "The *categorical* scale type … is equivalent to *ordinal* **except as a color scale**, where it provides a different default color scheme." ([Plot scales](https://observablehq.com/plot/features/scales))

**2.5 Tableau 10 (Maureen Stone).** Designed in CIELAB, "split … into a hue, chroma plane (CIELAB a*, b*) plus a lightness scale (CIELAB L*)". The rationale for ten is **nameability**, not just discriminability: "**A full-hue circle like the Tableau 10 is a good default because the colors are visually very distinct and correspond to simple color names like red, blue, purple, and brown.** … Tableau 10 contains 10 different hues, with varying chroma." ([Stone, 2016](http://web.archive.org/web/20220511053940/https://www.tableau.com/about/blog/2016/7/colors-upgrade-tableau-10-56782)). Stone deliberately breaks Brewer's equal-lightness rule — categorical palettes "tend to contain several distinct hues **and a range of lightness values**" — and moved defaults off red-green, "much more friendly to our users with common forms of color-vision deficiencies" (*ibid.*).

## 3. Answers

**Q1 — Is assigning hues to semantic roles the same operation as assigning hues to chart series?** **Yes, at the level of the encoding channel — and the literature has a name for it.** The unifying concept is that both are a **nominal-level variable mapped to the hue channel**; the modern name is Munzner's **identity channel** ("Identity Channels: Categorical Attributes … Color hue"), the classical name is Bertin's **selective, non-ordered retinal variable**, and the mechanism in grammar terms is Wickham's scale: "A scale controls the mapping from data to aesthetic attributes … Scales are common across layers to ensure a consistent mapping from data to aesthetics." Cleveland & McGill make the equivalence explicit by exclusion — hue is left out of the accuracy ranking because it has no "unambiguous single method of ordering from small to large and thus might be regarded as **better for encoding categories rather than real variables**." Tufte names the same job "**color as noun**", i.e. *to label*, and labelling a node "Error" and labelling a series "EMEA" are the same act. Bertin even supplies the architectural instruction directly: "**different views of the same data should be presented with the same values of variable at the selective level of organization**" — one palette, many views, is his rule, not a convenience. Operationally, every shipped library agrees: Vega-Lite routes *nominal* → `"categorical"` range → `tableau10`, and Observable Plot's *categorical* type "is equivalent to *ordinal* **except as a color scale**". A diagram node role and a chart series are both just nominal fields.

**Q2 — Where the equivalence breaks down.** The channel is the same; the **assignment** is not. Three documented breaks:
1. *Semantic resonance is measurable.* Lin, Fortuna, Kulkarni, Stone & Heer coin the term: "We use the term **semantically-resonant** to refer to color choices that are evocative of a given concept." Their study found "expert-chosen semantically-resonant colors improve speed on chart reading tasks compared to a standard palette" — 9.1s → 8.1–8.2s per task, Cohen's *d* ≈ 0.15–0.17, "more than 10% of the task-time"; and "resonant assignments helped most for the **concrete, non-arbitrary** categories", with iconic/arbitrary categories showing ~0.25s (little or none) ([EuroVis 2013](https://idl.cs.washington.edu/files/2013-SemanticColor-EuroVis.pdf)).
2. *The literature already names the split you care about.* Setlur & Stone: "Objects with strongly associated colors, like fruits, vegetables, political parties, and brands also benefit from semantic coloring. **In contrast, a chart of sales performance colored by region or sales team has no inherent semantic coloring.** An automatic system … must first determine the **colorability** of the objects in the category, then determine the appropriate coloring" ([TVCG 2016](https://infovis.fh-potsdam.de/readings/Setlur2016.pdf)). Error/Storage/Process are colourable; Series A/B/C are not. Also: "'apple' as a fruit is red or green, but 'apple' as a brand is white or silver gray" — resonance is *context-dependent*, so the same token can't be both.
3. *Viewers infer meaning even when you intended none.* Schloss et al.'s **assignment inference**: "to interpret color-coding systems, people perform assignment inference to determine how colors map onto concepts", and they solve it **globally** — "Participants discarded objects in bins that optimized the color-object associations of the entire set … even when there was a stronger associated option available" ([Cognitive Research 3:5, 2018](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s41235-018-0090-y)). So an *arbitrary* slot assignment is still read as meaningful, and a wrong-feeling hue costs you.

The conventions themselves are real but **not universal**. Ware's coding guideline 7: "Color-coding conventions must sometimes be taken into account. Some common conventions are: red = hot, red = danger … **However, it is important to keep in mind that these conventions do not necessarily cross cultural borders. In China, for example, red means life and good fortune, and green means death.**" (*Information Visualization*, ch.4). Empirically, colour–emotion associations are mostly-but-not-fully shared: "Pattern-similarity analyses revealed universal color-emotion associations (average similarity coefficient r = .88). However, local differences were also apparent" (Jonauskaite et al., [*Psychological Science* 31(10)](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/169496/)). No standard mandates red=error — WCAG only forbids colour as the *sole* cue — but design systems encode it as a token: GOV.UK ships `error = #ca3535` ("Use this colour for error messages") and pairs it with a visually hidden "Error:" prefix ([GOV.UK DS](https://design-system.service.gov.uk/components/error-message/)).

**Architectural consequence:** one hue-segmentation mechanism can legitimately serve both, *provided* semantic roles get **pinned** slots (red reserved for Error, etc.) and series get **rotated** slots from the remainder. Stephen Few's Rule #4 is the same instruction: "Use different colors only when they correspond to differences of meaning in the data" ([Perceptual Edge, 2008](http://www.perceptualedge.com/articles/visual_business_intelligence/rules_for_using_color.pdf)).

**Q3 — The practical ceiling on categorical hues.** Sources converge on **~7, hard-capped near 12**:

| Source | Number | Wording |
|---|---|---|
| Healey, IEEE Vis 1996 ([PDF](https://vis.cs.brown.edu/docs/pdf/Healey-1996-CEC.pdf)) | **7** | "**seven isoluminant colours is the maximum we can display at one time**, while still allowing rapid and accurate identification of any one of the colours" |
| Harrower & Brewer 2003, p.32 | 7 / 12 | "cartographers **seldom use more than seven classes**"; "maximum … ColorBrewer supports is 12" |
| Ware, *Information Visualization* ch.4 | **6–12** | "We can only use **between six and twelve color codes reliably.**" (his 12 map to the Berlin & Kay basic colour terms + cyan) |
| Munzner, VAD ch.10 slides sl.20 | 6–12 | "rule of thumb: **6-12 bins, including background and highlights**" |
| ggplot2 book 3e ch.11 | ~8 | "works well for up to about eight colours" |
| Wilke, *Fundamentals of Data Viz* ([pitfalls](https://clauswilke.com/dataviz/color-pitfalls.html)) | 3–5 / 8 | "work best when there are **three to five** different categories … Once we reach **eight to ten** … too burdensome" |
| Tufte, *Envisioning Information* p.81 | 20–30 | "more than 20 or 30 colors frequently produce not diminishing but **negative returns**" |

Note Healey's 7 is *isoluminant* — he adds that "It might be possible to use intensity in a controlled manner to increase the number of colours", which is exactly what Tableau 10 does (10 hues "with varying chroma" and "a range of lightness values").

**Beyond the ceiling, the recommendations are unanimous — change the channel, not the palette:**
- Group into "other": "If the size of the categorical domain exceeds the number of colors in the scheme, colors will be reused; **combining values into an 'other' category is recommended**" ([Observable Plot](https://observablehq.com/plot/features/scales)); "If you need more than seven colors in a chart, consider using **another chart type or to group categories together**" ([Datawrapper Academy](https://www.datawrapper.de/academy/what-to-consider-when-choosing-colors-for-data-visualization)).
- Direct-label instead of a legend: "**direct labeling instead of colors** when you need to distinguish between more than about eight categorical items" (Wilke); "label elements of the graph on the graph itself rather than making a separate color-coded key, since **matching same colors in distant places is extremely difficult**" ([Okabe & Ito](https://jfly.uni-koeln.de/color/)).
- Switch channel / facet: Munzner's Ch.10 asks "so what can we do instead?" and answers with the other identity channels — spatial region, shape ("many bins"), motion (VAD Ch.5 slides).

**Q4 — Colour as redundant vs sole encoding.** The normative rule is WCAG 2.2 SC 1.4.1 (Level A):
> "**Color is not used as the only visual means of conveying information**, indicating an action, prompting a response, or distinguishing a visual element." — [Understanding SC 1.4.1](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/use-of-color.html)

Its intent is explicitly *not* anti-colour: "This should not in any way discourage the use of color on a page, or even color coding **if it is complemented by other visual indication**" (*ibid.*). The chart-shaped worked example is a legend where "Sighted users who cannot perceive all the color differences can still understand the image by relying on the numbers" (*ibid.*). Companion rule for non-text marks: SC 1.4.11 requires "a contrast ratio of at least **3:1** against adjacent color(s)" for "**Graphical Objects** — Parts of graphics required to understand the content" ([Understanding SC 1.4.11](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/non-text-contrast.html)).

Prevalence: "Among populations with Northern European ancestry, it occurs in about **1 in 12 males and 1 in 200 females**" ([MedlinePlus Genetics, NIH/NLM](https://medlineplus.gov/genetics/condition/color-vision-deficiency/)); Okabe & Ito give "One in twelve Caucasian (8%), one in 20 Asian (5%), and one in 25 African (4%) males".

The visualisation-specific advice is *redundant encoding* — the same idea Wickham built into the grammar ("we can also create **redundant mappings**, mapping the same variable to multiple aesthetics … useful when producing a graphic that works in both color and black and white"):
- "Use not only different colors but also a **combination of different shapes, positions, line types and coloring patterns**, to ensure that information is conveyed to all users including those who cannot distinguish differences in color." — [Okabe & Ito, Color Universal Design](https://jfly.uni-koeln.de/color/)
- "Avoid encoding by hue alone • **redundantly encode** – vary luminance – change shape" — Munzner, VAD Ch.10 slides, sl.10.
- "encode data redundantly, using multiple different aesthetic dimensions" / "design your figures so they don't need a legend" — [Wilke, *Redundant coding*](https://clauswilke.com/dataviz/redundant-coding.html).
- "Using different **lightnesses** in your gradients and color palettes has the big advantage that readers with a color vision deficiency will still be able to distinguish your colors" — Datawrapper.

⚠️ **The sharpest finding for this architecture:** *categorical + colourblind-safe is the hardest combination and the primary sources say so.* Harrower & Brewer: "**Almost all of the qualitative schemes are difficult for colour-blind readers** and all of the sequential schemes are useful to them, because they include visible lightness differences" (p.34). ColorBrewer's shipped `blind` flags confirm it — only Dark2, Paired and Set2 are ever flagged safe, and only at 3 classes; at ≥5 classes **zero** qualitative schemes qualify ([colorbrewer_schemes.js](https://github.com/axismaps/colorbrewer/blob/master/colorbrewer_schemes.js)). ggplot2's `hue_pal()` says the same of itself ("does not generate colour-blind safe palettes"). Tableau's fix was to deliberately **break Brewer's equal-lightness rule** and move defaults off red-green. So: a hue-only segmentation mechanism is *expressively* correct (Q1) and *accessibly* insufficient on its own — lightness variation across the hue ring plus a second channel (shape/icon/label) is not optional decoration, it is the SC 1.4.1 requirement.
