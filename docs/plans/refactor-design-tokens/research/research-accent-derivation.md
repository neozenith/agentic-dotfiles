# Deriving a Second Accent from One Brand Colour — Research
Researched 2026-09-03. Every claim carries a source URL. Rationales asserted-but-never-published are flagged `[UNVERIFIED]`.

## 1. The classical schemes — definitions and angles

Angles are hue offsets on a **hue-as-angle wheel**; which wheel (RYB, HSV, LCh, OKLCH) is a free parameter that the classical statements never pin down.

| Scheme | Offset(s) from base | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Complementary | +180° | [Adobe Color wheel](https://color.adobe.com/create/color-wheel) |
| Split-complementary | +150°, +210° | "two colors flanking its complement" — [Adobe Color](https://color.adobe.com/create/color-wheel) |
| Triadic | +120°, +240° | [Adobe Color](https://color.adobe.com/create/color-wheel) |
| Tetradic / square | +90°, +180°, +270° | [Adobe Color](https://color.adobe.com/create/color-wheel) |
| Analogous | ±30° (typ.) | [Adobe Color](https://color.adobe.com/create/color-wheel) |
| Double-split-complementary, compound, monochromatic, shades | — | Adobe's remaining rules; [added 2018](https://community.adobe.com/t5/adobe-color-discussions/missing-color-rules-split-complementary/td-p/9702106) |

Adobe Color publishes **no rationale** for the angles — the help surface is a feature list, not a justification. `[UNVERIFIED]`

**The wheel these angles came from is RYB, and RYB is not a colour model of anything measurable.** Bruce MacEvoy: primary colours "are either imaginary, invisible 'lights' … or they are imperfect, real colorants that reproduce only some colors", and there is "no historical source prior to the 18th century that starts with three 'primary' or 'primitive' colors" ([handprint, *Do 'primary' colors exist?*](https://www.handprint.com/HP/WCL/color6.html)). Visual complements are "defined by a straight line from that hue through the white point" — a chromaticity-diagram fact, not a wheel-position fact (same page). Consequently the RYB complement of blue is **orange**, while the additive/light complement of blue is **yellow** ([Wikipedia, Complementary colors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complementary_colors)); the same page notes "traditional color theory has been superseded by empirically-derived modern color theory". So the wheel art education teaches gives a *different answer* from the wheel screens live on, for the single most-cited relationship.

## 2. Do the classical relationships survive in a perceptually uniform space?

**No — and this has been tested directly, in a perceptually uniform space, at scale.**

Tan, Echevarria & Gingold, *Palette-based color harmonization*, IEEE TVCG 31(10), 2025, [doi:10.1109/TVCG.2025.3546210](https://doi.org/10.1109/TVCG.2025.3546210) ([project page + PDF](https://cragl.cs.gmu.edu/harmonization/)) re-posed the classical templates in **CIE LCh** precisely to remove the HSV artefact — "Contrary to previous work using HSV color wheels, the LCh color space ensures that perceptual effects are accounted for with no additional processing" — then ran the first controlled perceptual tests of them (>1000 participants total).

Study 3 (N=100, MTurk, 60 archetypal palettes each paired against a hue-randomised control at fixed L\*=60, C=33, 3000 evaluations per template) — preference for the archetypal palette over random, 95% CI:

| Template | Spacing | Preferred over random |
|---|---|---|
| Monochromatic | 0° (L/C varied) | **67 – 70.3%** |
| Square | 90° | **58.1 – 61.6%** |
| Complementary | 180° | **40.7 – 44.2%** (loses) |
| Triad | 120° | **40.5 – 44%** (loses) |

Verbatim: "The monochromatic and square templates were perceived to be significantly more harmonic than random palettes… However, random templates were perceived as more harmonic than complementary and triad templates." And in the conclusion: "Perhaps the lack of a consistent parameterization of hue means only monochromatic, analogous, and complementary colors are grounded in the human visual system. **Triad, square, and complementary arrangements are dependent on the color-space.** … Our archetypal study found complementary (180°) and triad (120°) arrangements were disfavored." They also found participants **did not prefer harmonised images** to originals for any template.

Corroboration: Schloss & Palmer, *Aesthetic response to color combinations: preference, harmony, and similarity*, Atten Percept Psychophys 73(2):551–571, 2011, [doi:10.3758/s13414-010-0027-0](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13414-010-0027-0) ([PDF](https://palmerlab.berkeley.edu/pdf/Schloss&Palmer(2011).pdf)) — "pair preference and harmony both increase as **hue similarity** increases… Although pairs with highly contrastive hues are generally judged to be neither preferable nor harmonious, **figural color preference ratings increase as hue contrast with the background increases**." That is the whole design lesson in one sentence: contrast is for *figure vs ground*, not for *accent vs accent*.

O'Donovan, Agarwala & Hertzmann, *Color Compatibility From Large Datasets*, SIGGRAPH 2011 ([project page](https://www.dgp.toronto.edu/~donovan/color/)) tested "whether certain hues or hue templates may be preferred by viewers" against large rating datasets; Tan et al. cite it as finding that well-liked themes do **not** follow equi-angular hue distributions.

**The one counterweight, and it matters.** Forni, Darmon & Benzaquen, *Harmonious Color Pairings*, [arXiv:2508.15777](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.15777) (2026) collected a full 13-hue preference matrix and found "Higher preferences are observed in the contrast region (between 160° and 220°), flanked by regions of lower preference at smaller angular distances" — partial support for ~180°, and *none* for analogous ("the other preference regions they proposed — coined similarity … are not consistently supported"). But their headline is the destruction of the premise: "preferences are highly hue dependent, thereby challenging classical color harmony theories"; "the assumption of hue independence cannot be reasonably upheld." PCA gave a *bipartite* group structure, not a rotation rule. (They also ran in HSL and concede "HSL suffers from perceptual non-uniformity", recommending CIELAB/OKLAB for follow-ups.) The Forni-vs-Tan disagreement is not noise: Forni measured **preference for a pairing**, Tan measured **harmony**, and Schloss & Palmer showed those two dissociate. A contrast pair can be *liked* while being *un-harmonious*.

Wikipedia's [Harmony (color)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harmony_(color)): "the number of possible color combinations is virtually infinite thereby implying that predictive color harmony formulae are fundamentally unsound", citing Burchett (2002) and O'Connor, *Colour Harmony Revisited*, Color Research & Application 35(4):267–273, 2010 ([copy](https://www.academia.edu/404543/)), which holds the Munsell/Chevreul/Itten balance-of-complementaries claims "remain unsubstantiated". MacEvoy is blunter: "There is no reason to accept color theory claims for fixed complementary color schemes"; "The traditional primary triad — red, yellow, and blue — is obsolete and should not be taught" ([color wheels](https://www.handprint.com/HP/WCL/color13.html)); and on why one angle cannot be one relationship — "The 'complementary' contrast between purple and green is almost restful, the contrast between orange and cerulean is cheerful, the contrast between yellow and ultramarine is stark" ([color harmony](https://www.handprint.com/HP/WCL/tech13.html)).

### Three mechanical facts that survive regardless of harmony theory

1. **Hue *constancy* ≠ hue *spacing uniformity*.** Oklab was fit so hue does not drift when L or C change — that is the fix for CIELAB's blue→purple shift ([evilmartians](https://evilmartians.com/chronicles/oklch-in-css-why-quit-rgb-hsl)). Ottosson never claims equal hue-angle steps are equal perceptual steps; his own picker tables mark Okhsl/Okhsv "Varies Evenly: **no**" ([colorpicker](https://bottosson.github.io/posts/colorpicker/)). Popular OKLCH advocacy conflates the two. `[UNVERIFIED]` for the equal-steps claim — no primary source found.
2. **Hue angle is not a perceptual distance until weighted by chroma.** CIEDE2000: `ΔH′ = 2√(C₁′C₂′)·sin(Δh′/2)` ([Color difference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Color_difference)). A 180° rotation at C=0.02 and at C=0.25 are entirely different events. CSS Color 4 states the general form: "the angles and spacing corresponding to particular hues depend on the color space" ([W3C](https://www.w3.org/TR/css-color-4/)).
3. **Constant-L, constant-C rotation is the operation most likely to leave sRGB.** Ottosson's own example: "starting from light and saturated yellow in Oklab and rotating the hue to blue, that blue color does not exist in sRGB" ([Smashing interview](https://www.smashingmagazine.com/2024/10/interview-bjorn-ottosson-creator-oklab-color-space/)). Naive clamping destroys the equal-lightness property that justified OKLCH in the first place.

**Verdict for §2:** OKLCH fixes the *mechanical* objection to hue rotation — rotation there holds L and C, which HSV cannot — so `hue + N` is a *well-behaved operation*. It does not make `hue + 180` a **perceptual complement**: perceptual complements are defined by a line through the white point ([handprint](https://www.handprint.com/HP/WCL/color6.html)), Oklab's a/b axes were fit to IPT hue-uniformity data rather than to unique hues, and no colour scientist — Ottosson included — has asserted the equivalence. ColorAide, which defaults its harmony generator to OkLCh, justifies it only on hue consistency and gamut, then hedges: "we make no assertions that this is better than using any other color space… what is better or even harmonious can be largely subjective" ([ColorAide harmonies](https://facelessuser.github.io/coloraide/harmonies/)). Google agrees in code — see §3.

## 3. Material 3 / Material Color Utilities — the full variant table

From [`dynamic_scheme.ts`](https://github.com/material-foundation/material-color-utilities/blob/main/typescript/dynamiccolor/dynamic_scheme.ts) (2021 spec). `h` = source hue, `c` = source chroma.

| Variant | primary | secondary | tertiary | neutral | neutral-variant |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| `TONAL_SPOT` (default) | `h`, C36 | `h`, C16 | **`h+60`, C24** | `h`, C6 | `h`, C8 |
| `VIBRANT` | `h`, C200 | rotated (see below), C24 | rotated, C32 | `h`, C10 | `h`, C12 |
| `EXPRESSIVE` | `h+240`, C40 | rotated, C24 | rotated, C32 | `h+15`, C8 | `h+15`, C12 |
| `RAINBOW` | `h`, C48 | `h`, C16 | `h+60`, C24 | `h`, C0 | `h`, C0 |
| `FRUIT_SALAD` | `h−50`, C48 | `h−50`, C36 | `h`, C36 | `h`, C10 | `h`, C16 |
| `NEUTRAL` | `h`, C12 | `h`, C8 | `h`, C16 | `h`, C2 | `h`, C2 |
| `MONOCHROME` | `h`, C0 | `h`, C0 | `h`, C0 | `h`, C0 | `h`, C0 |
| `CONTENT` | `h`, `c` | `h`, `max(c−32, c×0.5)` | **analogous** (`TemperatureCache.analogous(3,6)[2]`, dislike-fixed) | `h`, `c/8` | `h`, `c/8+4` |
| `FIDELITY` | `h`, `c` | `h`, `max(c−32, c×0.5)` | **complement** (`TemperatureCache.complement`, dislike-fixed) | `h`, `c/8` | `h`, `c/8+4` |

`VIBRANT` rotation table: hues `[0,41,61,101,131,181,251,301,360]` → secondary rotations `[18,15,10,12,15,18,15,12,12]`, tertiary `[35,30,20,25,30,35,30,25,25]`.
`EXPRESSIVE`: hues `[0,21,51,121,151,191,271,321,360]` → secondary `[45,95,45,20,45,90,45,45,45]`, tertiary `[120,120,20,45,20,15,20,120,120]`.

Note what the rotation tables mean: **the offset is hue-dependent, not constant.** Google's own "vibrant" and "expressive" schemes rotate by 10–35° and 15–120° respectively *depending where on the wheel you start* — an explicit admission that a single fixed angle does not behave uniformly.

**Is `+60` justified anywhere?** Almost nowhere — and the one gloss that exists undercuts it. The constant appears bare and uncommented in every language port and in the AOSP predecessor (`ColorScheme.kt`: `const val ACCENT3_HUE_SHIFT = 60.0f`); AOSP commit messages attribute the numbers to an internal, never-published UX spreadsheet. Android documents only the shape — "Each variant has a unique recipe that transforms the seed colors… through vibrancy and rotating the hue" ([dynamic-colors](https://developer.android.com/develop/ui/views/theming/dynamic-colors)). MCU's own [`scheme_generation.md`](https://github.com/material-foundation/material-color-utilities/blob/main/concepts/scheme_generation.md) worked example uses a *different* number ("the tertiary palette shifts the hue by 30").

The single Google-authored sentence that explains 60° sits in the javadoc of [`SchemeContent.java`](https://github.com/material-foundation/material-color-utilities/blob/main/java/scheme/SchemeContent.java) — a variant that **does not use +60**: "Tertiary Container is an analogous color, specifically, the analog of a color wheel divided into 6, and the precise analog is the one found by increasing hue. **This is a scientifically grounded equivalent to rotating hue clockwise by 60 degrees.** It also maintains constant appearance." Read plainly: Google's position is that the *temperature-analogous* computation is the grounded thing, and `+60` is its naive approximation. (The sentence exists only in the Java/Kotlin ports; TS, Dart, Swift and C++ stop before it.) `[UNVERIFIED]` — treat `+60` as an undocumented designer constant.

**Google is moving off the fixed `+60`.** The 2025 spec (M3 Expressive, `DynamicSchemePalettesDelegateImpl2025`, same file) makes chroma a function of platform and dark mode, and replaces `TONAL_SPOT`'s constant rotation with a breakpoint table carrying **negative** rotations — hues `[0,20,71,161,333,360]` → `[−40,48,−32,40,−32]`, chroma 28 (phone) / 32 (watch). `EXPRESSIVE` also *lost* its `+240` primary rotation there. Only NEUTRAL/TONAL_SPOT/VIBRANT/EXPRESSIVE have a 2025 form; `maybeFallbackSpecVersion` pins MONOCHROME, CONTENT, FIDELITY, RAINBOW and FRUIT_SALAD to 2021 permanently. So the one shipped constant-angle rule in the industry is being retired in favour of hue-dependent tables.

**Google explicitly rejected `hue+180` as "complement".** [`temperature_cache.ts`](https://github.com/material-foundation/material-color-utilities/blob/main/typescript/temperature/temperature_cache.ts): the complement is "usually described as being across the color wheel", but "History of this shows intent as a color that is just as cool-warm as the input color is warm-cool" — so it searches the opposite hue arc for the colour whose *relative temperature* equals `1 − inputRelativeTemperature`, using Ou, Woodcock & Wright's LCh warm-cool model, `temperature = −0.5 + 0.02·C^1.07·cos((h − 50°)·π/180)`. Likewise `analogous` returns colours "equidistant in **temperature**", not equidistant in hue. This is the strongest single piece of evidence that a shipped, perceptually-grounded system found the naive angle inadequate.

**Derived hues get a taste guard.** [`dislike_analyzer.ts`](https://github.com/material-foundation/material-color-utilities/blob/main/typescript/dislike/dislike_analyzer.ts) flags "dark yellow-green that is not neutral" (hue 90–111°, chroma >16, tone <65) citing Palmer & Schloss on "universal distaste for dark yellow-greens", and `fixIfDisliked` lightens to tone 70. Any hue-rotation rule can land in that well; MCU applies the guard only on the `CONTENT`/`FIDELITY` derived tertiary.

Khroma's site was proxy-blocked from this machine; its findings rest on secondary sources quoting the creator directly.

The pattern across the table: **outside Google and Khroma, essentially nobody derives a second accent hue.** Systems either hand-pick every hue (Tailwind, Open Color, Radix, Carbon, Primer, Polaris) or derive only *ramps and neutrals* from the seed (Leonardo, Ant, Radix's gray rule). The sharper finding is what the OKLCH-native cohort does: **every one of them stops at constraining a second hue rather than choosing it** — equal lightness, equal chroma, equal contrast. Evil Martians' widely-copied relative-colour trick `oklch(from var(--error) l c 140)` is exactly this: it shows how to make an equally-bright sibling hue and says nothing about which angle to use ([OKLCH in CSS](https://evilmartians.com/chronicles/oklch-in-css-why-quit-rgb-hsl)). Meanwhile every harmony *implementation* inspectable in this survey — uicolors, atmos, Realtime Colors — runs in **HSL**, even where the product markets OKLCH. So there is solid published prior art for the *constraint* on a second accent and essentially none for the *angle*.

## 5. The contrarian case — one accent

The `~/foss/diagram-design` assertion under audit is `skills/diagram-design/references/style-guide.md:135` — "**One accent**: pick one color for `accent`. Two accents erases the focal signal."

Support in the literature:
- **NN/g** (Kelley Gordon, 2021-06-06): "Reserve the accent color for what you want to stand out the most on your page — for example, the primary call to action"; "A small number of colors reinforces visual hierarchy and contrast because there is less for users to consider and get distracted by", with a recommended cap of ~3 colours total ([nngroup.com](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/color-enhance-design/)).
- **Schloss & Palmer 2011** gives it a mechanism: the thing that makes a figure pop is *hue contrast against its ground*. A second accent adds a second figure, not a stronger one ([doi](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13414-010-0027-0)).
- **Tan et al. 2025** gives the aesthetic cost: "strong contrast was perceived to be disharmonious", and complementary/triad pairings lost to random ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1109/TVCG.2025.3546210)).
- **60-30-10** is the folk version — 10% accent — and its most-cited failure mode is exactly the diagram-design claim: an accent used everywhere stops directing attention ([UX Planet](https://uxplanet.org/the-60-30-10-rule-a-foolproof-way-to-choose-colors-for-your-ui-design-d15625e56d25)). Origin is interior design, not perception research; no empirical backing published. `[UNVERIFIED]`
- **Tufte**: "Above all else show the data"; colour "should be used with restraint and purpose, not decoration" — the data-ink argument against decorative hue ([Envisioning Information ch.5, *Color and Information*](https://blogs.ischool.berkeley.edu/i247s12/files/2012/02/Chapter-5-Envisioning-Information-Tufte.pdf)).
- **Shopify Polaris is the strongest single precedent — a major design system with deliberately *no* accent at all:** "The Shopify admin interface adopts a black and white color scheme, intentionally creating a neutral backdrop… Using color as decoration is exclusive to illustration." ([Polaris colors, via archive](https://web.archive.org/web/2025/https://polaris.shopify.com/design/colors) — the live page now redirects to shopify.dev).
- **IBM Carbon** puts the same policy in a positive form: "The core blue family serves as the primary action color… **Additional colors are used sparingly and purposefully.**" ([usage](https://carbondesignsystem.com/elements/color/usage/)).
- **Atlassian publishes the prohibition outright** — "**Avoid mixing different accent colors**" ([accents](https://atlassian.design/foundations/color/accents)).

Where it is *not* supported — the position is stricter than the mainstream:
- **Refactoring UI** (Wathan & Schoger) recommends "one or maximum two primary colors" plus "three or four accent colors" — but those accents are *semantic* (destructive, warning, positive), not a second brand hue ([summary](https://updivision.com/blog/post/book-review-refactoring-ui-by-adam-wathan-steve-schoger)).
- **Atlassian**'s 10 accent hues are defined by the *absence* of meaning: "Use accent tokens for any experience where the color is just one of many colors, and **changing it would have no impact on the experience**… Don't use accent tokens when the color has a specific meaning" ([accents](https://atlassian.design/foundations/color/accents)). A categorical/user-content palette, not a focal signal — so it does not contradict one-focal-accent, and its own mixing prohibition supports it.
- **M3** ships a tertiary in every scheme, i.e. a second derived accent is always present even if unused.

So: the *reasoning* ("two accents erases the focal signal") is better supported than expected — NN/g states it as a hierarchy claim, Schloss & Palmer supply the mechanism, and Polaris, Carbon and Atlassian all publish some form of the restriction. The *absoluteness* remains stricter than mainstream practice, but it is no longer idiosyncratic. **No source found anywhere argues that a second accent improves hierarchy.**

## Answers

**1. If a brand states exactly one colour, what is the best-attested rule for a second accent hue?**
`hue + 60°` at chroma 24 against a chroma-36 primary — Material 3 `TONAL_SPOT` and `RAINBOW`, i.e. what every Material Theme Builder / `ColorScheme.fromSeed` consumer gets ([dynamic_scheme.ts](https://github.com/material-foundation/material-color-utilities/blob/main/typescript/dynamiccolor/dynamic_scheme.ts)). But "best-attested" means *most-shipped*, not *most-justified*, and two facts temper it: Google's only written gloss calls `+60` the naive stand-in for the real thing — "the analog of a color wheel divided into 6… **a scientifically grounded equivalent to rotating hue clockwise by 60 degrees**" ([SchemeContent.java](https://github.com/material-foundation/material-color-utilities/blob/main/java/scheme/SchemeContent.java)) — and the 2025 spec replaces `TONAL_SPOT`'s constant with a hue-breakpoint table carrying negative rotations. **If you want the version with research behind it, take the thing `+60` approximates**: MCU's temperature-analogous tertiary (`CONTENT`) or its temperature complement (`FIDELITY`), both on Ou/Woodcock/Wright ([temperature_cache.ts](https://github.com/material-foundation/material-color-utilities/blob/main/typescript/temperature/temperature_cache.ts)). Avoid `+180` and `+120`: both were measured as *less* harmonious than random hue pairs in LCh ([doi:10.1109/TVCG.2025.3546210](https://doi.org/10.1109/TVCG.2025.3546210)). Radix's rule — pick the *gray* "saturated with the hue closest to your accent hue" ([composing-a-palette](https://www.radix-ui.com/colors/docs/palette-composition/composing-a-palette)) — is the same-hue answer: the second colour is a tinted neutral, offset 0°.

**2. Are classical colour-wheel relationships valid in OKLCH?**
**The rotation is valid; the relationships are not.** In OKLCH/LCh a hue rotation preserves lightness and chroma, which HSV rotation cannot — Tan et al. adopted LCh for exactly this reason, and it is the entire real benefit. It does not make 180° a complement: a perceptual complement is a line through the white point, not an angle ([handprint](https://www.handprint.com/HP/WCL/color6.html)); the RYB wheel supplying the 180° figure disagrees with the additive wheel about blue's complement ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complementary_colors)); "the angles and spacing corresponding to particular hues depend on the color space" ([CSS Color 4](https://www.w3.org/TR/css-color-4/)); and angle is not a perceptual distance until chroma-weighted ([ΔH′](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Color_difference)). Tested *in* LCh, complementary (40.7–44.2%) and triad (40.5–44%) lost to random hue pairs — "Triad, square, and complementary arrangements are **dependent on the color-space**" ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1109/TVCG.2025.3546210)). The only classical rule with measured support is a broad **160–220° contrast band** for pair *preference*, and the same paper concludes hue-independence "cannot be reasonably upheld" ([arXiv:2508.15777](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.15777)). Google's own code declines to define complement as `h+180`. **Treat classical angles in OKLCH as tuned constants, not theory — and call the result a contrast pair, not a complement.**

**3. Does any system make the second accent optional / trigger-derived?**
**One does, and its trigger is contrast.** Khroma runs a *separate* model for two-colour pairings precisely because of a constraint the four-colour model doesn't have — "The reason why they're two distinct algorithms is because **the two color one needs a minimum contrast restriction**" ([interview](https://magazine.designmatters.io/how-khroma-uses-machine-learning-to-create-endless-color-palettes/)); the threshold and metric are unpublished `[UNVERIFIED]`. Otherwise, no surveyed system withholds a second hue until something demands it. Closest analogues: M3 always materialises a tertiary but scopes its *use* to a trigger ("contrasting accents that balance primary and secondary… or bring heightened attention to an element" — [how-the-system-works](https://m3.material.io/styles/color/system/how-the-system-works)); MCU's `CONTENT`/`FIDELITY` derive tertiary from the source's own temperature and pass it through `fixIfDisliked`, a conditional correction ([dislike_analyzer.ts](https://github.com/material-foundation/material-color-utilities/blob/main/typescript/dislike/dislike_analyzer.ts)); Radix derives only a *gray* companion, and its custom generator takes accent and gray as independent inputs. A demand-driven derivation (emit `accent-2` only when a spec declares two simultaneous focal roles) has **no published precedent** — it would be novel, is consistent with everything in §5, and Khroma is the one datapoint suggesting the trigger should be a *contrast requirement* rather than a count.

**4. Strongest published argument for exactly one accent.**
NN/g, stated as a hierarchy claim rather than a taste claim: "A small number of colors reinforces visual hierarchy and contrast because there is less for users to consider and get distracted by", paired with "reserve the accent color for what you want to stand out the most" ([nngroup.com](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/color-enhance-design/)). The mechanism is Schloss & Palmer's: hue contrast raises preference for a *figure against its ground*, while raising it between two accents lowers both pair-preference and harmony ([doi:10.3758/s13414-010-0027-0](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13414-010-0027-0)) — one accent buys the figure-ground effect; two spend it against each other. The strongest *existence proof* is Shopify Polaris, a major system that ships no accent at all: "The Shopify admin interface adopts a black and white color scheme, intentionally creating a neutral backdrop… Using color as decoration is exclusive to illustration" ([archive](https://web.archive.org/web/2025/https://polaris.shopify.com/design/colors)) — with Carbon ("Additional colors are used sparingly and purposefully") and Atlassian ("Avoid mixing different accent colors") publishing weaker forms of the same restriction.
